How a paper gets a red flag

A red flag is a request for a person to compare two papers. This is everything that happens before we make that request.

Walk the five steps What a flag means

By Ziqian Zhang · OpenLAIR · about 7 minutes

The problem

New words, same results

Paper laundering rewrites earlier public work so it reads as new: the same results, tables or contributions in different words, without a clear citation of the source. Language models make that rewriting cheap.

Illustration, not a real case

Earlier paper, 2024

We train a 12-layer encoder with a masked-span objective on 3.1M sentence pairs. On the held-out split it reaches 84.7 F1, 2.3 points above the strongest baseline.

Submission

Our approach pre-trains a twelve-block transformer encoder by reconstructing hidden spans across 3.1 million paired sentences. Evaluated on unseen data, it attains an F1 of 84.7, surpassing the best prior method by 2.3.

Shared content words: 4, and no shared phrase
Same claims and numbers: 5 of 5

Similarity checkers such as iThenticate look for copied wording, and here there is almost none to find. What carries over is the substance: the method, the data and every number. That is what our checks compare.

What a flag means

Four labels, one of them a flag

Every screened paper ends with one label. Only one of them asks a person to act, and none of them is a finding of misconduct. Turn a card to read what it means.

Never a red flag on its own

the paper's own preprintshared authorsa cited dataset or baselinereuse that is clearly attributed

The system can miss these cases, which is why every flag goes to a person before anything else happens.

The five steps

From submission to a request for review

Each step has one job, one kind of output, and one typical way to fail. The line runs on its own; pick a station to stop on it. Later posts take one step each.

1submission PDF
Step 1

Read the paper

Submission PDF→Text, tables and numbers, each with its page and position

Every later step points back to a place in the paper, so the text keeps its page positions. Tables matter most: a laundered paper often keeps its numbers even when every sentence is new.

Can go wrongA misread table cell shifts a number, and a real match stops matching.

Not covered by its own post yet
Step 2

Pull out what can be checked

Full text→Claims, method steps, results and table rows

A paper's contribution lives in a small number of claims and results. These are what we compare with earlier work, and they are what the live page shows as extracted claims.

Can go wrongA claim that is never extracted is never checked.

Planned
Step 3

Find candidate sources

The paper→A short list of earlier public papers that might be its source

Our first live version gathered about 138 candidates per paper, screened their abstracts with a small, cheap model, and sent 3 on to full-text comparison. Papers dated after the submission do not count as sources.

In Part 1 we rebuilt this step around a full-text library of 2.92M arXiv papers. On the public PAN200 benchmark, a true source reaches the top 3 for 91% of papers, against 74.5% before.

Can go wrongA source that is not found here cannot be recovered by any later step.

Step 4

Compare side by side

The paper and one candidate→Matching passages, numbers and tables, located on both sides

Each of the 3 candidates is read in full next to the submission. The output is evidence a person can check: this passage here matches that passage there. A comparison costs us about $0.005 per pair.

Can go wrongA cited baseline or a shared author's earlier work can look like reuse.

Part 2 · in progress
Step 5

Label it, then hand it to a person

Evidence from every pair→One label for the paper

The evidence from all pairs becomes one of the four labels. When the closest match is the paper's own preprint, the label says so. A flag is where the system stops and a person starts.

Can go wrongTurning many small matches into one label is a judgment, and judgments can be wrong. Anyone can ask for a correction.

Why a funnel

Read most candidates briefly, and only 3 in full

Screening a whole conference has to be cheap per paper. Most candidates only need their abstract read by a small model. Only the few that survive get a full-text comparison.

How we measure it

One step at a time, and end to end

A step can be good while the whole pipeline is not, and the other way round. We test both, and each post says which kind of number it reports.

One step: does it do its job?

For finding sources we use PAN200, the public test set of the PAN 2026 Generative Plagiarism Detection task. It is large and public, so other groups can run the same test.

200 heavily rewritten papers593 known sources2.92M papers to search

It answers one question: is the true source put in front of the next step? It cannot say whether the final label is right.

End to end: is the label right?

Our diagnostic benchmark holds 20 anonymized ICLR 2027 papers with 34 target–source pairs and a human label for every paper, with the evidence locations.

16 No source finding2 Same paper (preprint)2 Source review recommended

One annotator, two positives, and selection influenced by models. It does not measure how common source reuse is or how accurate a detector is in general. About the benchmark

Fine print

What we share, and how to correct us

A screening system works partly because authors do not know exactly how to evade it, so some details stay private. Everything a reader needs to judge a result does not.

We share

  • What each step does, what it costs and how we measure it.
  • Every third-party method we compare with, named and cited.
  • Our failures, in the posts on each step.

We keep private

  • Prompts, model choices beyond their size, and thresholds.
  • How queries are built from a paper.
  • Code and our internal test cases, until after the review period of the conference we screen.
