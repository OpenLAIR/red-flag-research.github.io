Paper laundering rewrites earlier public work into a submission that looks new. The wording changes, but numbers, method steps and claims stay close to the original. Our checker compares a submission with a few candidate sources in depth. A source that retrieval never surfaces is never compared, so retrieval recall caps the recall of the whole system. v1 put a true source in its top 3 for 74.5% of benchmark papers. v2 was built to close that gap without slowing the screening down.

How it works

Search wide, then let a small model choose

v2 does two things. It runs one cheap search over a big library, then gives a small judgment to a short list.

2.92Mfull-text arXiv papers
1

Search the full text, not the abstract

A lexical first stage searches the full text of 2,919,718 arXiv papers. Laundering changes the framing, so a source's title and abstract often look nothing like the target's. The reused material is in the body: setups, numbers, method details.

Searching titles and abstracts alone reaches Hit@3 0.660. Searching the full text with the whole paper as one query reaches 0.235, because a long query dilutes the useful terms. How we build queries from a paper is one of the details we keep private (see below).

2

Keep 16 candidates

The first stage keeps the top 16 documents. For at least 93.5% of PAN200 targets, a true source is already among those 16.

The pool is the ceiling for everything after this step, and we tested whether a bigger pool raises it in practice. It doesn't (lesson 2).

3

A source selector picks the likely sources

A 4B-parameter open-weight model reads the target and, for each candidate, only its title and the opening of its abstract. It gives each candidate a probability of being a source, and the top 3 go on to side-by-side comparison.

It needs no candidate full text and costs about $0.00011 per paper. On PAN200 it lifts Hit@1 from 0.645 to 0.900. It was not trained on these benchmarks.

Two settings: Flash and Max

v2 Flash (redflag-retr-v2-flash) is the default when throughput matters. v2 Max (redflag-retr-v2-max) spends about three times as many searches on long papers, and it is the one we will keep improving. The two differ only in how first-stage queries are drawn from the paper.

Results

200 rewritten papers, one library of 2.92M

PAN200 is the public test set of the PAN 2026 Generative Plagiarism Detection task. Each query is a full paper produced by heavily rewriting one or more arXiv papers. Every system searches the largest library it can use in practice; none gets a small pool that is known to hold the answers.

The hard part

Long papers hide the reuse deep inside

PAN200 queries are short. Real submissions are long, and a launderer can put reused blocks anywhere. Our own benchmark had hidden a blind spot here.

An earlier version of our first stage only read about the first 15% of a long paper. Every one of our 41 synthetic cases had reuse outside that window.

What went wrong on the way

Most of what we learned came from things that did not work. Each lesson here moved our numbers by more than many of the ideas we tried.

01

An index merge quietly lowered recall

Same code, same settings, fewer sources found.

+
Top-16 pool 0.920 → 0.900Hit@1 0.805 → 0.785

We merged the main index and six supplements into one. Two explanations failed: the settings were identical, and searching deeper made the pool worse (0.880). The cause was BM25 document-length normalisation computed separately in each shard. A library-wide fix lifted the pool to 0.945 and Hit@3 to 0.935, but it lowered Hit@1 and doubled first-stage time, so neither setting uses it yet.

An infrastructure change needs the same before-and-after test as a method change.

02

A bigger pool raised the ceiling and lowered accuracy

More candidates gave the selector more ways to be wrong.

+
64 candidates: ceiling +5.5 ptsHit@1 −14 ptsRecall@3 −16 ptsGPU 4.7×

Going the other way did not help either. With 8 candidates, Hit@1 fell 5 points and GPU time dropped only about 20%. The selector was not more accurate with fewer distractors (85.0% correct when a source was in the pool, against 87.2% with 16).

A ceiling tells you how much you could gain, not whether the next stage can use it.

03

Reranking a large pool bought almost nothing

14 hours of fetching and 3–4 GPU-hours for one point.

+
JLP, interleave +0.010 Hit@3Dense only −0.060Unit-level BM25 −0.215 Hit@1

We reranked the first stage's full pool (median 268 candidates) with four scorers. All four needed the full text of about 40,000 candidate documents. A dense reranker we tried later needed a median of about 100 seconds per paper on a GPU, so we stopped it.

Spend compute on a strong judgment over a small pool, not on reranking a large one.

04

Our own benchmark had a blind spot

Short queries hid a window that covered 15% of a long paper.

+

On PAN200 the earlier first stage looked fine. On full-length papers it only saw the opening. We found the problem only after building 41 synthetic full-length cases with reuse placed throughout the text. See the long-paper section.

Test on inputs shaped like the real ones, not just the public benchmark.

05

We mislabeled a closed-pool result

An early table called title search the best system at rank 1.

+

That row came from a run whose index had been built from the benchmark's own small source corpus, which contains every answer by construction. Its notes called it open-domain. We caught it by tracing every row back to the manifest of the index it queried, rescored it on the 3.2M-record arXiv metadata index (Hit@3 0.660), and dropped every closed-pool row.

The label on a result is not evidence of its search space.

06

Web search was the most expensive and among the weakest

About $0.13 a query for Hit@3 0.395.

+

We extracted the literature-search step of the CMU Paper Reviewer agent: one LLM call writes queries, and a web-search API runs them. Web results often point to pages that cannot be matched to an arXiv source, and a few short queries cannot carry the detail that full-text matching uses. The full agent completed one paper in 1,827 seconds and ranked the true source 17th.

For this task, what you search matters as much as how you search it.

Fine print

What we publish, and what we keep back

A screening system works partly because authors do not know exactly how to evade it. We name and cite every third-party method, and we keep the details of our own pipeline private.

We keep private

  • How first-stage queries are built from a paper: which parts are used, term selection, weighting and how results are merged.
  • Which model the selector is, beyond its size, and its prompt and settings.
  • How the full-text index is built and maintained, beyond the length-normalisation finding above.
  • Thresholds, code and our internal synthetic cases, until after the review period of the conference we screen.

Limits of these numbers

  • The comparison rests on one public benchmark of machine-generated rewrites. Real laundering may look different.
  • There is no held-out split, and the comparison systems got no comparable tuning, so our numbers are likely optimistic.
  • Every true source here is in the library. In practice a source may be outside arXiv or newer than our snapshot.
  • Retrieval is not detection. A retrieved source is a lead for a person to examine, not a finding about any paper.
